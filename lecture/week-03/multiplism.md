# Part 1: Critical Multiplism

## 1. Introduction to Critical Multiplism
Critical multiplism is a research stance that deliberately uses multiple, diverse methods to test a question from different angles. When separate approaches converge on the same finding, confidence in the result grows because shared conclusions are less likely to be artifacts of a single method’s biases.

### 1.1 Why it matters in analytics
Organizations rely on analytics to inform decisions that should generalize beyond the original sample or time period. By triangulating across methods (e.g., surveys + interviews; experiments + observational studies), generalizability and predictive accuracy improve.

### 1.2 Mixed methods in practice
Qualitative methods (e.g., focus groups, interviews) provide context, mechanisms, and lived experience. Quantitative methods (e.g., surveys, descriptive and inferential statistics) provide breadth and standardized measurement. A pragmatic workflow often uses both—e.g., a broad survey to map patterns followed by focus groups to interpret surprising or nuanced results.

### 1.3 Key takeaways
- No single method is inherently superior; each has strengths and blind spots.
- Triangulation supports validity, not just reliability.
- Plan method combinations to probe the most consequential alternative explanations within resource constraints.

## 2. Multiple Operationalism (Multiple Measures of a Construct)
Multiple operationalism means measuring an abstract construct (e.g., engagement) with several complementary indicators rather than a single metric. Because complex constructs are multi‑dimensional, any one indicator is incomplete; using several reduces construct under‑representation and single‑indicator bias.

### 2.1 Example: Student engagement
Engagement spans cognitive, behavioral, and emotional facets. Administrative indicators like attendance, course enrollment intensity, and disciplinary incidents capture slices of this construct. Combining such indicators yields a broader, more faithful signal.

### 2.2 Ways to combine indicators
- Use families of similar dependent variables.
- Build composite indices.
- Model latent variables (e.g., factor analysis/SEM).

### 2.3 Benefits and cautions
- Diversifies measurement bias and enhances generalization.
- Prevents organizational myopia from optimizing to a single metric.
- Requires transparent documentation of how each measure maps to the construct.

## 3. Proxies (Stand‑ins for Difficult‑to‑Measure Constructs)
A proxy is a variable used as an approximate indicator of a construct that is hard or costly to measure directly. Proxies are associated with the target construct but do not fully embody it.

### 3.1 Everyday examples of proxies
- Attendance as a proxy for student engagement.
- Years of education as a proxy for cognitive ability.
- BMI as a proxy for body fat; GDP per capita as a proxy for quality of life.

### 3.2 Choosing proxies wisely
- Clarify whether the proxy is an outcome of the construct or merely correlated.
- Prefer parsimony without sacrificing validity.
- Make limitations explicit so the proxy is not mistaken for the construct.

### 3.3 Proxies vs. demographic stand‑ins
Demographic variables can reveal between‑group differences, but uncritical use risks superficial or stigmatizing narratives. Mechanism‑focused composites often explain outcomes more meaningfully.

**Case illustration:**  
A composite of adaptation resources (economic marginality, domestic workload, family relations, religiosity, response to illness, social support) explained more variance in health outcomes than ethnicity alone.

---

## 4. Putting It Together: Practical Guidance

### 4.1 Designing a study with critical multiplism
- Start from the threats your primary method cannot rule out.
- Select complementary methods that stress‑test those threats.
- Pre‑plan what counts as convergent evidence.

### 4.2 Building a measurement plan
- Map each construct to multiple indicators.
- Pilot and check reliability.
- Consider latent‑variable modeling when feasible.

### 4.3 Using proxies responsibly
- Justify the proxy’s link to the construct.
- Monitor for proxy drift.
- Prefer mechanism‑based composites over demographic stand‑ins.

---

## 5. Key Definitions (Quick Reference)
- **Critical multiplism:** Using multiple, diverse methods to test the same question.
- **Multiple operationalism:** Measuring a construct with several indicators.
- **Proxy variable:** A correlated stand‑in for a difficult‑to‑measure construct.
- **Triangulation:** Agreement of findings across different methods or measures.

# Part 2: Unobtrusive Measures (from *Approximations to Knowledge*)

## 1. Purpose of the Reading
- Interviews and questionnaires are intrusive and biased.
- Unobtrusive measures broaden methodological options.
- Multiple methods are needed to cross‑validate findings.

## 2. Examples of Unobtrusive Measures
- Erosion measures  
- Accretion measures  
- Physical responses  
- Behavioral traces  
- Archival indicators  

## 3. Operationism and Multiple Operations
- Single operational definitions are insufficient.
- All measures contain irrelevant variance.
- Triangulation strengthens validity.

## 4. Interpretable Comparisons & Plausible Rival Hypotheses
- Experimental design  
- Index numbers  
- Rival hypotheses  

## 5. Internal vs. External Validity
- **Internal validity:** whether differences are real.  
- **External validity:** whether findings generalize.  

## 6. Sources of Invalidity
- Respondent error  
- Investigator error  
- Sampling error  

## 7. The Outcropping Model
Multiple imperfect indicators collectively validate a theory.
